Principal Component Analysis

In 1901 Karl Pearson asked which line or plane lies closest to a cloud of points, measured by the sum of squared perpendicular distances [1]. In 1933 Harold Hotelling asked which combinations of variables carry the most variance, and named them principal components [2]. Both questions have the same answer: the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix.

1 · The best view

Real data rarely comes as two tidy columns you can plot. Here we start with three: a cloud of points in 3D. To draw it on a flat screen you have to project it onto a plane, and some planes show the shape far better than others. The question is which plane keeps the most variance, the total spread of the points around their mean. Rotate the cloud and hunt for it yourself, then let PCA find it. PCA's answer is the plane spanned by the first two principal components, and the direction it throws away is the one with the least variance.

2 · Unsupervised

Most machine learning people hear about is supervised: you give the algorithm examples together with the right answers, and it learns to predict the answer for new examples. PCA works differently. It gets only the data matrix X, one row per observation and one column per measured variable, with no labels at all. Whatever it finds, it finds from how the columns vary and move together. Switch between the two modes to see what each kind of method is given.

3 · The best line

This is the core of PCA in its simplest form. Take points with two variables and project each one onto a line through their mean. The projections have a variance, and that variance depends on the angle of the line. Turn the line yourself and watch the number change, then press Find PC1. The best line is the first principal component. It is also the line Pearson was after in 1901, the one with the smallest total squared distance to the points: maximum variance and least squares give the same answer.

MathDerivation

For centered points xi (i = 1…n), the projection on a unit vector w is wTxi, and its variance is wTCw. Maximizing this with ‖w‖ = 1 (Lagrange multiplier λ) gives Cw = λw [2]. Since |xi|² = (projection)² + (distance to line)², maximum variance is the same as minimum squared distance [1].

4 · The algorithm

Section 3 found PC1 by searching. In practice nobody searches, because PCA is a short calculation. Here it is one step at a time on 60 points: subtract the mean, build the covariance matrix, find its eigenvectors and eigenvalues, rotate the data onto them, and keep only the components you need. Every number in the equations below is computed live from the points on screen, so you can follow the arithmetic as the picture changes.

What you're looking at

Same idea, smaller data: 60 dots with two numbers each. The open circle is their average. The dashed ellipse is a picture of the covariance matrix, roughly the outline that holds most of the dots. The arrows are its eigenvectors, orange for PC1 and blue for PC2, longer when there's more spread that way.

Up to step 4 the axes are the original measurements. At step 5 the whole picture turns so the axes become PC1 and PC2, and at step 6 everything is flattened onto PC1.

CodePython
import numpy as np
Xc = X - X.mean(axis=0)
C = Xc.T @ Xc / (len(X) - 1)
lam, W = np.linalg.eigh(C)
lam, W = lam[::-1], W[:, ::-1]
Z = Xc @ W
kept = lam[:1].sum() / lam.sum()

In practice the singular value decomposition of X̃ is used instead; it gives the same eigenvectors [3].

5 · Ten protein simulations

This is where PCA earns its place in computational chemistry. A molecular dynamics simulation records every atom's position in every frame, which quickly adds up to thousands of numbers per frame. Caves, Evanseck and Karplus (1998) ran ten simulations of the small protein crambin, each started with different initial velocities, and used PCA on the backbone atoms to compare them [4]. The runs settled into a few distinct conformational regions, and pooling all the runs gave an average structure closer to the X-ray structure than any single run did. The toy model here is built to behave the same way: 46 Cα atoms and ten runs of 80 frames. PCA gets all 800 frames mixed together and is never told which run a frame came from.

Ten 120 ps simulations of crambin at 300 K from different initial velocities. PCA of the backbone showed four conformational regions, each run mostly confined to one. The pooled average structure was closer to the X-ray structure than any single-run average [4]. The model above is invented to reproduce this behavior; frames are pre-aligned, as required for PCA of coordinates (essential dynamics) [5]. For reviews of PCA on MD data see [6].

6 · Mixture spectra

PCA isn't only for structures. Here each observation is an absorption spectrum measured at 200 wavelengths, and every sample is a different mixture of the same few dyes. By Beer–Lambert, each spectrum is a weighted sum of the pure dye spectra, so the data really vary in only as many directions as there are dyes; everything else is noise. PCA can count those directions without being told what the dyes are or how many there are [7]. Raise k to rebuild a spectrum from more components and watch where adding more stops helping.
